## A few things worth spending time verifying

**Track record**
A provider that's stayed in stable operation for a year or two has probably weathered
several rounds of network-environment shifts and line disruptions — that survival
itself is a useful signal. A brand-new provider with no history isn't automatically
unusable, but it's not something you'd want to rely on as your sole main line.

**Whether monthly billing is offered**
If a provider only offers annual plans priced noticeably below the equivalent
month-by-month rate, that's worth a second look — see [Red flags](./red-flags).
Providers that let you buy month-to-month, or try a small amount first, carry
relatively more manageable risk.

**Refund / support policy**
Whether the refund rules are spelled out, and how to reach support if something goes
wrong — if this basic information is missing or vague, that absence is itself a signal.

**Account registration requirements**
If a provider insists on a phone number or ID verification instead of a simple email
signup, it's worth pausing to consider how that information might be misused down the
line. A reasonable approach is to use a separate overseas email address (not tied to
anything domestic) and avoid attaching sensitive information to the account.

**Whether the line description is specific**
Providers willing to clearly state "leased line / relay / direct" and roughly where
their servers are located are more trustworthy than ones that only use vague phrases
like "premium optimized" or "enterprise-grade lines." Vague language is often designed
to let you imagine something fancier than what's actually running.

## Things easy to get misled by

**"We use residential IPs"**
As covered in the [Glossary](./glossary), a fair share of these claims are inaccurate.
Don't take the provider's word alone — cross-reference against lookup tools like
ip2location for a rough read, but don't treat any single tool's score as an absolute
standard either.

**"98% IP purity" / "extremely low risk score"**
There's no unified industry standard for scoring "IP purity" — every platform uses its
own custom scoring system, and the same IP can get wildly different scores on different
tools. Rather than fixating on these numbers, it's more useful to just check whether
the node actually works for the specific site or service you plan to use it for.

**"IPLC/IEPL leased line" at a price far below market rate**
Real leased-line bandwidth is expensive. If a plan is marketed as a "leased line" but
priced well below what comparable leased-line products typically cost, it's probably
just an ordinary relay wearing a fancier label.

**"Unlimited data"**
Bandwidth and servers both have real costs. A promise of "unlimited data" usually means
either there's a throttling fallback hiding behind it, or the business model itself
can't last long.

**Latency numbers alone don't tell you much about speed**
Low latency doesn't mean fast throughput, especially since relay-based providers and
direct-connection providers are best measured with different speed-test methods
(using the wrong method gives misleading numbers). If you want to dig into the
differences in test methodology, it's worth checking the provider's own documentation
or asking support what testing method they recommend — different line types genuinely
need different measurement approaches to produce meaningful numbers.

## A simple rule of thumb

Rather than getting hung up on a specific score or marketing claim, the more practical
approach is: buy the cheapest plan available (monthly, or an even shorter cycle) and
actually use it for a while under the conditions you'll really be in — the sites you
regularly visit, the hours you're usually online. That tells you far more than any
marketing page ever will.
